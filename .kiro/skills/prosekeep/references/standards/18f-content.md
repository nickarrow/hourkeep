# 18F content guide: audience, voice and page structure

Source: [18F Content Guide](https://guides.18f.org/content-guide/), especially [plain language](https://guides.18f.org/content-guide/our-approach/plain-language/), [conciseness](https://guides.18f.org/content-guide/our-approach/be-concise/), [content structure](https://guides.18f.org/content-guide/our-approach/structure-the-content/), and [technical and interface writing](https://guides.18f.org/content-guide/our-style/technical-and-interface-writing/). The 18F guide is a U.S. government work released under [CC0](https://guides.18f.org/content-guide/license/). Prosekeep is an independent adaptation, not an official 18F product or endorsement.

## Start with the user's purpose

- Identify who needs this page, what they are trying to find or accomplish, and the decision or action available to them.
- Put the most useful answer or next step near the beginning. A compelling essay can establish a problem first; service and task pages should prioritize the user's task.
- Prefer concrete objects, actors, and actions. Name the service, the request, the document, the fee, and the outcome instead of a general process or transformation.
- Avoid language that assumes the reader understands internal systems or organizational history. Explain genuinely necessary technical terms on first reference.

## Keep the right voice

- Write with authority grounded in what is known. Straightforward, conversational language works for specialist as well as general audiences.
- Contractions are appropriate when they fit the author's voice and the project's style.
- Avoid pompous nouns and abstract verbs. Replace abstract promises with observable work and outcomes.
- The author and document type determine tone. A researcher may qualify an uncertain conclusion; a technical specification needs exact states; a founder may make an argument in the first person.
- Do not turn these practices into a universal requirement to sound like a federal website.

## Make pages scannable

- Headings tell readers what the section covers or establishes. Ensure the claim made in the heading is supported below it.
- Use a meaningful hierarchy. For HTML, use actual heading elements; styling is not a substitute for structure.
- Place important content before secondary detail. Keep paragraphs focused; use lists for actual sets of comparable items or steps.
- Use descriptive link text that identifies the destination or action. Avoid relying on surrounding visual context alone.
- Prefer content in context to giant FAQ pages that repeat material dispersed elsewhere.

## Explain technology and tasks

- Call buttons, links and fields by their actual names. Check the current interface before writing instructions.
- Use verbs for instructions. Separate steps where a person has to take distinct actions, and describe the result they can observe.
- Define what a system has done and what it has not confirmed. Do not write a completion claim from a request or acknowledgment alone.

## Editorial questions

1. Can a new reader understand the central point from the lead and headings?
2. Are claims supported by what the product or evidence can actually demonstrate?
3. Is the page written for its audience rather than for its owner's internal taxonomy?
4. Can someone using only headings or links understand their purpose?
5. Are necessary technical details retained and unnecessary organizational jargon removed?
