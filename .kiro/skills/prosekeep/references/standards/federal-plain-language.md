# Federal plain-language principles

Sources: [Digital.gov plain-language guide series](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language) (adapted from the former PlainLanguage.gov guidance), [18F plain language](https://guides.18f.org/content-guide/our-approach/plain-language/), and [CPSC's summary of federal principles](https://www.cpsc.gov/About-CPSC/Policies-Statements-and-Directives/plain-language-principles). The Plain Writing Act requirement applies to covered federal communications, not automatically to private publishing.

## Think about the audience

Write down the reader, what they already know, and what they need to do. Multiple audiences may need distinct pages or clearly distinguished paths. Check real questions and user research when available. Do not pretend to have tested comprehension unless testing took place.

## Organize by usefulness

Place the answer, action, or most consequential fact where the reader will find it quickly. Move exceptions after the general rule, unless the exception governs the user's situation. Use headings that carry subject matter, not decorative labels. Organize related information together.

## Write sentences people can act on

Use the active voice when it clarifies responsibility. Name who performs an action. Prefer ordinary verbs to nominalizations such as "implementation of" or "facilitation of." Keep the subject and verb close. Shorter sentences generally help, but do not cut essential legal or technical qualifications.

## Check the meaning

- Define specialized terms if the intended reader is unlikely to know them.
- Keep necessary statutory terms and exact names; explain them without replacing legally meaningful language with inaccurate approximations.
- Do not alter the force of "must," "may," or "should" during editing.
- Preserve deadlines, eligibility conditions, exceptions and appeals processes.
- Verify that the page enables the reader's intended action. A reading-grade score is neither a substitute for comprehension testing nor a universal acceptance threshold.

## Review method

Ask what readers will understand after one pass, where they will hesitate, and how that could be tested with representative people. Flag claims that depend on user research, accessibility testing or subject-matter review as unverified until that evidence exists.
