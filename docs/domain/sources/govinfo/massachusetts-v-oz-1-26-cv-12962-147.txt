In the context of administrative law litigation,
“a motion for summary
judgment is simply a vehicle to tee up a case for judicial review.” Boston

“[A]n inquiring court must review an agency action not to determine whether
a dispute of fact remains but, rather, to determine whether the agency action
was arbitrary and capricious.” Id. Because this standard “affords great
deference to agency decisionmaking and agency actions are presumed valid,
‘judicial review [under the APA], even at the summary judgment stage, is
narrow.’” California v. Dep’t of Educ., 2026 WL 2754968, at *11 (D. Mass.
Sept. 17, 2026) (alteration in original), quoting Associated Fisheries of
Maine, Inc. v. Daley, 127 F.3d 104, 109 (1st Cir. 1997).
I. APA Claims (Counts I and II)
The States assert two claims under the APA: (1) that the definition of
“medically frail” in the IFR is contrary to law (Count I); and (2) that its
definition, as well as the twelve-month lookback period and the self-
attestation limit, are each arbitrary and capricious (Count II).
a. Contrary to Law
The parties agree that, under H.R. 1, the term “medically frail” must
“includ[e]” individuals who are blind or disabled; have a substance use
disorder; have a disabling mental disorder; have a physical, intellectual or
developmental disability that significantly impairs their ability to perform
one or more activities of daily living; or have a serious or complex medical
6
[[page 7]]

condition. 42 U.S.C. § 1396a(xx)(9)(A)(ii)(V). Their dispute centers on
whether CMS was permitted to add a further limitation to the term in the
IFR, defining an individual who is “medically frail” as one who falls within
these five subcategories and “whose physical, mental, or other behavioral
health condition significantly impairs the individual’s ability to comply with
the community engagement requirement.
”
“As usual, when tasked with interpreting a statute, we ‘must start with
the text of the statute itself,’ aiming as best we can ‘to effectuate
congressional intent.’” Washington v. Dep’t of Hous. & Urb. Dev., 171 F.4th
473, 490 (1st Cir. 2026), quoting Teles de Menezes v. Rubio, 156 F.4th 1, 12
(1st Cir. 2025). “Unless the statute specifies otherwise, ‘we generally assume
that the [text] carries its plain and ordinary meaning.’” Washington, 171
F.4th at 490 (alteration in original), quoting City of Providence v. Barr, 954
F.3d 23, 31 (1st Cir. 2020).
Although H.R. 1 generally gives CMS discretion to define “medically
frail or otherwise has special needs,” the statute is clear that the term must,
“includ[e]” individuals within five listed subcategories.4 “[T]he verb to

In sum, the court agrees with the States that the agency lacked

portion of the IFR imposing a limitation that the individual’s “physical,

is beyond the powers of CMS (ultra vires) and thus contrary to law. Ryan v.
Immigr. & Customs Enf’t, 974 F.3d 9, 19 (1st Cir. 2020) (“When an agency

II. Spending Clause Claim (Count III)
Count III asserts that the IFR violates the Spending Clause of the U.S.
Constitution primarily because it does not provide clear notice of the States’
obligations to satisfy the conditions to receive the restricted federal Medicaid
funding. Given the court’s conclusion that the definition of “medically frail”
is contrary to law, it need not address this alternative ground for relief.

III. Relief
Having determined that the portion of the IFR imposing a requirement
that, to qualify as “medically frail,” an individual must establish that their
“physical, mental, or other behavioral health condition significantly impairs
the individual’s ability to comply with the community engagement
requirement” in addition to falling within one of the five enumerated
subcategories is contrary to law, the court must now determine the
appropriate remedy.
The court agrees with the States that vacatur of the offending language
is warranted here. The text can easily be excised from the IFR without
otherwise impacting the validity of its provisions, and vacatur (as opposed to
the Government’s preferred remedy of remand) ensures that the issue is not
left unresolved and that the parties have the clarity needed to comply with
the January 1, 2027, implementation deadline imposed by Congress.

The States have met their burden of showing irreparable harm for
which monetary damages would provide inadequate compensation – they
face the burden of costly and intensive alterations to their eligibility
verification systems. See Doe v. Trump, 157 F.4th 36, 79 (1st Cir. 2025).
They have also met their burden on the balance of hardship and public
interest factors. It is well-recognized that there is an inherent public interest
in ensuring that the government follows the law, and because enforcement
of an unlawful definition of “medically frail” may lead to the disenrollment
of otherwise eligible vulnerable individuals relying on Medicaid coverage for
continuity of care, the States (and public) face potential for great hardship in
the absence of an injunction. The court accordingly will issue the permanent
injunction requested by the States.
